What a world model is actually for

“World model” is used to mean at least three different things, and the disagreements that follow are usually not about the model at all — they are about which of the three someone has in mind.

The confusion is worth taking seriously because the three uses put genuinely different demands on the same machinery. A model asked to produce what a scene looks like is being graded on pixels; one asked to produce how a quantity evolves is being graded on a trajectory; one asked to produce what to do next is being graded on a choice out of a finite menu. Same world underneath, three incompatible notions of a good answer. It is tempting to treat these as three points on one spectrum of fidelity. They are not: they differ in the kind of object they emit, and that difference survives every amount of scaling.

Set out this way, the failure modes separate too. A renderer that is temporally inconsistent produces a strip of plausible frames that do not belong to the same shot. A simulator that is inconsistent produces a trajectory with a discontinuity in it — visible immediately, because a continuous signal has nowhere to hide a jump. A planner that is inconsistent produces an action sequence that is individually reasonable and jointly incoherent: grasp, grasp, place. Each pathology is legible only against the grammar of its own output type, which is a good argument for keeping the three apart in evaluation even when one network serves all three.

None of this settles which function matters most, and the honest answer is probably that it depends on what sits downstream. What it does settle is that “does the model understand the world?” is underspecified until you say which of the three you are asking it to do — and that benchmarks inherited from one of them transfer to the others less often than their names suggest.
